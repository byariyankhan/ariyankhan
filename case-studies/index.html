About these case studies

What you will find here

Each case study follows one edit from raw material to finished video: what made it hard, which decisions I took, and why. They are written for creators, educators and anyone curious about how an explainer or documentary is put together, not as advertisements.

Both are honest about what they are. The Chile study is about my own YouTube documentary. The talking-head study is an anonymised, representative scenario built from common problems in expert-led videos, so it names no client and shows no confidential footage. Neither claims view counts, revenue or audience-retention numbers.

What they have in common

Four ideas behind both edits

  1. Find the structure before cutting. The first pass is about meaning: the central question, the examples that move it forward and the parts that repeat it.
  2. Give the viewer an anchor. In a geography film that is the map; in a lesson it is the question the video promises to answer.
  3. Show what the voice says. Dates, numbers and section titles appear on screen at the moment they matter, so nothing important depends on catching one sentence.
  4. Change the picture for a reason. Maps, footage, labels and cutaways alternate to keep the pace, but every change supports the explanation instead of decorating it.
What each one covers

Inside the two studies

The Harsh Geography of Chile

An eight-minute documentary on a very long, very varied country. It covers opening on a visual paradox, using chapters to control how much information arrives at once, making maps behave like moving evidence, typography as an explanation layer, and an ending that returns to the central idea.

A 35-minute expert recording

A dense, single-camera lesson reshaped into a video with a visible structure. It covers finding the real narrative before making cuts, visual rhythm built around ideas rather than movement, small repeatable signals for each section, and the sound and captions that make it accessible.
