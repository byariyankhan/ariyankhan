Why So Much of Spain Is Empty

Spain is famous for its coasts and cities, yet much of its interior is almost empty. How geography, the Reconquista and a great twentieth-century migration created “emptied Spain”.

By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor

Spain is one of the most visited countries on Earth, famous for its beaches, its cities and its food. Fly over it, though, and you see something else: a huge, high, dry interior with very few people in it. Around 90% of Spaniards live on roughly 30% of the country’s land, mostly in Madrid and along the coasts. Across much of the interior, villages are ageing, schools are closing, and thousands of hamlets have been abandoned altogether. Spaniards even have a name for it: la España vaciada, “emptied Spain”.

How did one of Europe’s largest and best-known countries end up so empty in the middle? The answer lies in its geography, in the way it was resettled during the Middle Ages, and in a great migration only two generations ago.

01 A Country Built Like a Fortress

Spain covers about 506,000 square kilometres, making it one of the largest countries in the European Union, and it is one of the most mountainous countries in Europe. At its heart is the Meseta Central, a vast plateau that sits on average around 600 to 700 metres above sea level and is ringed and crossed by mountain ranges: the Cantabrian Mountains in the north, the Iberian System in the east, the Sierra Morena in the south and the Central System running right through the middle.

That height and those mountains shape the climate. Much of the interior has a harsh continental climate: long, hot, dry summers and cold winters, with far less rain than the green north or the mild Mediterranean coast. Soils across large areas are thin, and water is scarce. It is land that can support dry farming, vineyards and grazing, but not the dense populations of a fertile river valley.

The coasts face the sea and the world. The interior faces a high, dry plateau, and for most of history that made all the difference.

02 The Reconquista and a Land of Small Villages

The pattern of settlement in the interior goes back to the Middle Ages. From the eighth century, much of the Iberian Peninsula was ruled by Muslim states, and over the following centuries the Christian kingdoms of the north pushed south in the long process known as the Reconquista.

As they advanced, the kingdoms had to fill the land they took. They did it through repoblación, resettlement: granting land and privileges to settlers who would hold the frontier. The result, especially in Castile and Aragon, was a landscape of thousands of small villages spread across the plateau, each farming or grazing the land around it. That is why Spain today has more than 8,000 municipalities, and why so many of them are tiny.

Grazing mattered enormously. For centuries the Mesta, a powerful guild of sheep owners founded in 1273, moved huge flocks of merino sheep across the interior between summer and winter pastures. Castile’s wool made it rich, but a pastoral economy needs lots of land and few people.

03 The Great Rural Exodus

For all its difficulties, the interior was still full of people a century ago. What emptied it was not an invasion or a plague, but a migration within living memory.

In the 1950s and 1960s Spain industrialised quickly. Factories and jobs grew in Madrid, Barcelona, the Basque Country and other cities, while farming was mechanised and needed far fewer hands. Millions of Spaniards left the countryside, either for the cities or abroad, to France, Germany and Switzerland. Whole generations moved out, and they rarely came back.

The villages they left behind grew older and smaller. With fewer young people, fewer children were born; schools, shops, doctors and bus routes closed; and with fewer services, even more people left. It became a cycle that is very hard to break.

04 How Empty Is It Today?

The numbers are striking:

  • Most of Spain’s population lives in a handful of places: the Madrid region, the Mediterranean coast from Catalonia to Andalusia, the Basque Country, and the islands.
  • Around three in five Spanish municipalities have fewer than 1,000 inhabitants.
  • In the mountains of the Iberian System, across parts of Soria, Teruel, Cuenca, Guadalajara and Zaragoza, researchers have described a region with fewer than eight people per square kilometre, lower than parts of Lapland. They call it the “Southern Lapland”.
  • Thousands of villages and hamlets across Spain now have no permanent inhabitants at all.

The contrast with Madrid is extreme. The capital, at the geographic centre of the country, has grown into a metropolitan area of about seven million people, drawing in young people and investment from the very regions that are emptying around it.

05 Why It Matters

An empty interior is not just a curiosity. Older, smaller communities are expensive to serve, and the people who stay can find themselves hours from a hospital or a secondary school. Abandoned farmland and forests are more exposed to wildfires. And for many Spaniards, the loss of the villages their families came from is also a loss of culture, dialect and memory.

It has become a political issue too. In 2019 a citizens’ platform called Teruel Existe, “Teruel exists”, won a seat in the Spanish parliament on a single message: that the emptied interior had been forgotten.

06 Can Spain Fill It Again?

Spain’s governments have made the “demographic challenge” an official priority, with national plans to improve internet coverage, transport and services in rural areas. Some villages offer cheap housing or even free land to families who move in, and the growth of remote work, helped by Spain’s digital nomad visa, has brought a few newcomers back.

The results so far are modest. Geography has not changed: the plateau is still high, dry and far from the coast. What may change is what living there means, if fast internet and remote work let people earn a city income in a village.

07 Two Spains on One Map

Spain’s emptiness is the product of a high, dry plateau, of medieval resettlement that scattered small villages across it, and of a twentieth-century exodus that drained them. The crowded coasts and the empty interior are two sides of the same story.

Next time you look at Spain on a map, remember that the famous beaches and busy cities are only the edge of it. The middle holds one of the emptiest landscapes in Western Europe.
