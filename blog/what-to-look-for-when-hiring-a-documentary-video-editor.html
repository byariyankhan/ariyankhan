What to Look for When Hiring a Documentary Video Editor

A software list on a resume tells you nothing about whether someone can find the story buried in your footage.

Aug 16, 2026 5 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Video Editor & Documentary Creator
Rec 00:00:00:00

Hiring a documentary editor is a different decision than hiring someone to cut a talking head video or a Reel. The stakes are higher — you're usually handing over hours of interviews, archival material, and footage you spent real time and money capturing, and trusting someone to shape it into a story that didn't exist yet. Getting that hire wrong doesn't just cost you a mediocre video. It costs you the story.

01 A Reel That Matches What You're Actually Making

A flashy highlight reel full of fast cuts and motion graphics tells you someone can edit a sizzle reel. It doesn't tell you they can sit with two hours of interview footage and figure out what the story is actually about. Ask specifically for documentary or long-form samples — not a general demo reel — and watch how the story is built, not just how it looks. Does the pacing breathe? Does the structure make sense without narration holding your hand through it?

02 They Ask About Story Before They Ask About Footage

In a first conversation, notice what a candidate asks about first. Someone who leads with "what codec is it in" and "how many hours of footage" is thinking about logistics. Someone who asks "what's the story you're trying to tell" and "who is this for" is thinking like an editor, not an operator. Both questions matter eventually — but the order tells you what they actually prioritize on a project.

Software is a tool you can teach in a weekend. Knowing what to cut from a two-hour interview isn't.

03 Real Experience With Interviews and B-Roll Ratios

Documentary editing lives or dies on how well someone handles interview footage — finding the usable soundbites buried in rambling answers, trimming without losing the speaker's voice, and knowing when a cutaway saves an awkward edit versus when it just papers over a weak moment. This is a specific skill, different from cutting a scripted video where every line is already tight. Ask how they typically approach a rough assembly from raw interviews — the answer will tell you if they've actually done this before.

04 Restraint With Color, Music, and Graphics

Documentary work rewards subtlety. An editor who defaults to heavy color grades, dramatic music stings, and constant lower thirds — the kind of toolkit built for corporate promos or YouTube explainers — can make a documentary feel manipulated instead of honest. Look for a portfolio where the craft supports the story quietly. If you notice the editing before you notice the story, that's usually a sign it's doing too much.

05 A Clear, Realistic Revision Process

Documentary edits go through more structural changes than most video types — a scene that felt essential in the rough cut often gets cut entirely once the story tightens. Ask upfront how revisions work: how many rounds are included, how feedback is handled, and what happens if the story needs to be restructured after the first cut. An editor who has a clear answer has done this enough times to know it happens. One who seems surprised by the question probably hasn't.

— — —

A Few Red Flags

  • No long-form samples, only short-form or ad work — the editing muscles are genuinely different.
  • Can't explain a structural decision in their own reel — if they can't say why a scene is where it is, they may not have made that call themselves.
  • Promises a fixed turnaround for footage they haven't seen yet. Documentary timelines depend heavily on how usable the raw material is.

06 The Simplest Filter

If you only have time for one question in an interview or a message exchange, ask this: "Tell me about a project where the story changed significantly during the edit — what happened, and how did you handle it?" Anyone who has genuinely edited documentaries has a real answer to that. It's the one part of the job that can't be faked with a good reel.
