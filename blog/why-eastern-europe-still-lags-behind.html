Why Eastern Europe Still Lags Behind, and How Fast It Is Catching Up

Three decades after the fall of the Berlin Wall, wages east of the old Iron Curtain are still far below the West’s. How history made the gap, and why it is finally shrinking.

Sep 30, 2026 5 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

More than thirty years after the fall of the Berlin Wall, a line still runs through the middle of Europe. It is no longer made of barbed wire, but you can see it in the numbers: wages, productivity and household wealth in the countries of the former Eastern Bloc remain well below those of Germany, France or the Netherlands. A factory worker in Bulgaria earns a fraction of what the same work pays in Germany, and millions of Eastern Europeans have moved west to close that gap themselves.

Yet the same region has also been one of the fastest-growing parts of the world economy. Why does Eastern Europe still lag behind, and how quickly is it catching up? The answer runs through geography, several centuries of history and forty years of communism.

01 Which “Eastern Europe”?

“Eastern Europe” usually means the countries that were under Soviet domination after the Second World War: Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and the three Baltic states, together with the countries of the former Yugoslavia, and further east Ukraine, Belarus and Moldova. They are very different places, but most share a history of arriving late to industry, and then of having their economies rebuilt from Moscow.

02 A Late Start

The gap is older than communism. From the sixteenth century, while serfdom was fading in Western Europe, landowners east of the Elbe tightened their grip on the peasants who worked their estates, a pattern historians call the “second serfdom”. Much of the region exported grain to the West and stayed rural. Serfdom was only abolished in the Austrian Empire in 1848 and in the Russian Empire in 1861.

Industry, railways and big cities came later than in Britain, France or western Germany, and the region was split between empires, Austrian, Russian, Prussian and Ottoman, that often treated it as a borderland. When those empires collapsed in 1918, the new states that replaced them had a short and turbulent independence before the next war.

03 A Battlefield Twice Over

Geography made things worse. Much of the region lies on the North European Plain and the plains of the Danube, flat land with few natural barriers, between Germany and Russia. In the Second World War it became the main battlefield of the war in Europe. Poland lost around a sixth of its pre-war population; cities such as Warsaw were almost completely destroyed; and the Holocaust annihilated the Jewish communities that had been central to the region’s towns, trade and professions.

04 Forty Years of Central Planning

After 1945 the Soviet Union imposed communist governments across the region. Economies were run by central plans instead of markets: the state owned the factories, set prices and decided what to produce.

Central planning did build heavy industry quickly, and it brought mass education and literacy. But it had deep, lasting flaws:

  • Factories were built for quotas and for the Soviet bloc market, not for quality or for customers.
  • With no competition, technology fell steadily behind the West, especially in consumer goods and later in computing.
  • Shortages were normal: people queued for basic goods and waited years for a car or a flat.
  • Trade was locked into the Soviet-led bloc, Comecon, cutting the region off from Western markets and investment.

By the 1980s the gap in living standards with Western Europe was wide, and still growing.

05 The Painful 1990s

When communism collapsed in 1989 and 1991, the region had to rebuild market economies almost from scratch. Poland chose “shock therapy” in 1990: prices were freed overnight and state subsidies cut. Across the region output collapsed, inflation soared and unemployment appeared for the first time in decades, as old factories built for the Soviet market lost their customers overnight.

The paths then split. Poland, Czechia, Slovenia and the Baltic states built working institutions and recovered within a few years. Others, including Romania and Bulgaria, went through longer, deeper crises. Further east, in Ukraine and Russia, privatisation handed much of the old state economy to a small group of politically connected owners, the oligarchs.

06 Joining the European Union

For most of Central and Eastern Europe, the turning point was joining the European Union: eight countries in 2004, Romania and Bulgaria in 2007, and Croatia in 2013. Membership brought access to the world’s largest single market, EU funds for roads, railways and modernisation, and, above all, foreign investment. Western car makers, electronics firms and suppliers built factories to take advantage of skilled workers who cost less than in the West.

The catch-up since then has been real. Measured by output per person, adjusted for prices, Poland has gone from roughly half the EU average when it joined to around four-fifths today, and Czechia and Slovenia are close to the average. Poland was the only EU economy to avoid a recession in 2009.

07 Why the Gap Hasn’t Closed

If growth has been so strong, why does the region still feel poorer? Several reasons stand out:

  • The assembly line. Many of the most valuable companies are foreign-owned, and much of the region’s industry assembles products designed elsewhere. Profits and research often stay abroad, and wages follow.
  • Emigration. After the borders opened, millions of young, educated people left for Germany, Britain, Italy and Spain. Bulgaria’s population has fallen from about nine million in 1989 to under seven million, and Romania has several million citizens working abroad.
  • Ageing. Low birth rates plus emigration mean fewer workers supporting more pensioners.
  • Institutions. Corruption and weak rule of law remain problems in parts of the region, discouraging investment and entrepreneurship.
  • War. Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 brought war back to the region’s eastern edge, with higher energy prices, refugees and new defence spending.

08 Catching Up, Slowly

Eastern Europe’s story is not simply one of failure. It started later, was ruined twice by war, spent forty years locked in a system that could not keep up, and has since grown faster than almost anyone in Europe. The gap is shrinking; it just started very wide.

Next time you see the old Iron Curtain line on a map of European wages, remember that it is a line of history, not destiny, and that on the eastern side of it, the numbers have been moving in one direction for thirty years.
