Why France’s Geography Is So Powerful, and Its One Fatal Weakness

Mountains on three sides, three seas and a great river network made France a power for two thousand years. One flat stretch in the north almost undid it, until France closed it with diplomacy.

By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

France sits between the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea. It is the third-largest country in Europe, after Russia and Ukraine, and within its borders are ocean coastlines, the continent’s highest mountains, green river valleys and some of the richest farmland in the world. That variety is not just scenery. For two thousand years it has shaped what France could become: a great power, and at its height a global empire. The same geography also hid a weakness that let invading armies reach Paris again and again.

This is how geography made France powerful, where its one fatal gap lay, and how France finally closed it, not with a wall, but with diplomacy.

01 The France You Don’t See on the Map

Mainland France, the “hexagon” most people picture, borders eight countries: Belgium, Luxembourg, Germany, Switzerland, Italy, Monaco, Andorra and Spain. Ask which of its land borders is the longest, and most people guess Spain or Belgium. The answer is Brazil. French Guiana, on the northern coast of South America, is a full part of France, and its border with Brazil runs for about 730 km, longer than the frontier with Spain.

French Guiana is only one piece of “overseas France”: islands in the Caribbean, the Indian Ocean and the Pacific, from Martinique and Réunion to New Caledonia and French Polynesia, plus Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon off the coast of Canada. Together these territories make up roughly 18% of France’s land and around 97% of its sea area, because each island brings a vast ocean zone with it. About 2.8 million French citizens live there. Thanks to them, France has one of the largest maritime zones of any country in the world.

02 Gaul: Why This Land Was Worth Taking

Around 2,500 years ago this region was Gaul, home to the Celtic peoples the Romans called Gauls. They had chosen well: fertile river valleys, a mild climate and natural defences made them prosperous farmers and craftsmen, and made Gaul one of the richest regions of Europe.

That wealth drew Rome. In 58 BC Julius Caesar began the Gallic Wars. Forests, mountains and rivers, and the resistance of many different tribes, made the conquest long and bloody; the decisive siege of Alesia came in 52 BC, and by about 50 BC Gaul was Roman. It became one of the empire’s wealthiest provinces.

After the Western Roman Empire collapsed in 476, power in Gaul passed to the Franks. Under their king Clovis, who united most of the Frankish peoples around the year 500, the story of the kingdom that would become France began.

03 From Kingdom to Global Empire

Over the following centuries French rulers used the land’s advantages to build one of the largest empires in history. At its peak, between the two world wars, the French colonial empire covered nearly a tenth of the world’s land, more than twenty times the size of France itself, and ruled over roughly 110 million people across Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, the Caribbean and the Pacific.

Most of that empire became independent in the twentieth century, but the ties did not vanish. Fourteen countries in West and Central Africa still use one of the two CFA franc currencies, whose exchange rate is pegged to the euro and backed by a guarantee from the French Treasury, and French remains an official language across much of the continent.

04 Nature’s Walls: the Pyrenees, the Alps and the Rhine

Look at mainland France and three of its sides are protected by nature.

  • The Pyrenees run the length of the border with Spain. After Umayyad armies conquered most of the Iberian Peninsula in the early 700s, they raided north across these mountains for years, until the Frankish leader Charles Martel defeated them near Tours and Poitiers in 732. The battle marked the northernmost point of their advance into Western Europe.
  • The Alps, Europe’s highest mountain range, stretch across eight countries and guard France’s south-eastern border with Italy and Switzerland.
  • The Rhine separates France from Germany along the border of Alsace, a wide river that armies have always found hard to cross.

05 The Fatal Gap in the North

The fourth side is different. In the north-east, between the Rhine and the English Channel, France opens onto the North European Plain: flat, open land running through Belgium into Germany, with no mountains or great rivers to stop an army. From there the road to Paris is short.

German armies used that route three times in seventy years. In 1870 Prussia and its allies invaded, defeated France and besieged Paris. In 1914 the German army swept through Belgium and reached the Marne, within about 50 km of the capital, before it was stopped. In 1940 German tanks came through the Ardennes and across the plain, and France fell in six weeks.

Mountains had protected France for two thousand years. The one flat door in the north cost it two defeats and four years of trench warfare.

06 Closing the Door with Diplomacy

After the Second World War, France chose a new answer to its oldest security problem. Instead of building a bigger wall against Germany, it bound the two countries together. In 1950 the French foreign minister Robert Schuman proposed pooling French and German coal and steel, the raw materials of war, under a shared authority. The European Coal and Steel Community was founded in 1951, and it grew into the European Economic Community and eventually the European Union.

The goal, in Schuman’s words, was to make war between the two countries “not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible”. Seventy-five years later, a war between France and Germany is exactly that: unthinkable. It may be the most successful solution to a geographical weakness in modern history.

07 Three Seas and a Great Port

France’s coasts are just as important as its mountains. In the north-west it faces the English Channel, one of the busiest shipping lanes in the world, through which much of northern Europe’s trade passes. France is also the only European country with coastlines on the North Sea, the Atlantic and the Mediterranean.

From the Mediterranean, French trade can go three ways: west through the Strait of Gibraltar to the Americas, east through the Turkish straits to the Black Sea, and south-east through the Suez Canal to the Middle East, Asia and Australia. Goods coming the other way arrive at Marseille-Fos, France’s largest port, and travel up the Rhône valley into France and the rest of Europe. The long Atlantic coast opens directly onto North and South America.

08 The Rivers That Feed and Power France

France’s real secret weapon may be its rivers.

Rhôneabout 810 kmFlows from the Swiss Alps to the Mediterranean, carrying trade and powering industry.

Rivers like these helped give France the largest farmland of any country in the European Union, and made it one of the world’s leading food exporters: its agricultural and food exports are worth tens of billions of euros a year, from grain to wine and cheese. They also generate electricity: hydropower is France’s second-largest source of power after nuclear.

09 Rich Neighbours

Geography has also placed France in one of the wealthiest neighbourhoods on Earth. Switzerland, Luxembourg, Belgium, Germany and Monaco are among the richest countries in the world per person, and together with its other neighbours they are France’s main export markets. When the people next door have money to spend, it shows in your own economy.

Today France is one of the world’s largest economies and a leading power in Europe. It is the most visited country in the world, welcoming around 100 million international tourists a year, and its wine, food and luxury goods are known everywhere.

10 Geography Sets the Stage

From the fertile valleys of the Gauls to the European Union, France’s story shows that geography shapes a country’s destiny, but doesn’t decide it alone. Mountains and rivers gave France its strength; a flat northern plain gave it its greatest weakness; and in the end it was diplomacy, not geography, that closed the gap.

Next time you look at France on a world map, remember that the hexagon is only part of it, and that its most important border was secured by a treaty, not a mountain.
