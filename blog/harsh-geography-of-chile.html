The Harsh Geography of Chile: Why Most Chileans Live in the Middle

Chile is one of the longest, narrowest countries on Earth, yet about three-quarters of its people live in a small central slice of it. Here is how history and geography made it that way.

Sep 30, 2026 6 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:08:02:00

Chile sits between the Pacific Ocean and the Andes, a ribbon of mountains, lakes, deserts and coastline about 4,270 km (2,653 miles) long and, on average, only around 177 km (110 miles) wide. It is one of the longest and narrowest countries on Earth. You might expect a country with that much variety, and that much good weather, to be settled from end to end. It isn’t. Roughly three in every four Chileans live in the central regions between Valparaíso and Biobío, around the capital, Santiago, which together cover only about 15% of the national territory. Much of the rest is home to mining towns, farms, fishing villages and small border communities, spread very thinly.

So why is so much of Chile so empty? The answer is a mix of colonial history, war and some of the hardest geography any country has to live with. This is the story behind Chile’s shape, and behind where Chileans ended up living.

01 People Arrived Here Very Early

Humans have lived in what is now Chile for a very long time. At Monte Verde, in the south, archaeologists found a settlement dated to about 14,500 years ago, one of the oldest known sites of human life in the Americas. Its people lived in small groups, surviving by hunting, fishing and gathering plants.

By the time Europeans arrived, the land was home to many indigenous peoples. Three of the best known are:

  • The Mapuche, in central and southern Chile, the largest group, living mostly from farming.
  • The Aymara, in the high plateau of the far north, near today’s borders with Peru and Bolivia.
  • The Kawésqar, canoe-going people of the channels and islands of the far south.

Many smaller groups lived alongside them, from the Atacama in the north to Tierra del Fuego in the south.

02 Spain Arrives, and the Mapuche Fight Back

In 1541 the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Valdivia founded Santiago. Spain’s new colony, later governed as the Captaincy General of Chile, took indigenous land, forced conversions and put local people to work for the colonists.

The Mapuche resisted. The long, intermittent conflict that followed is known as the Arauco War, and it lasted, on and off, for most of the colonial period. Spain was never able to conquer the Mapuche heartland south of the Biobío River, which for centuries served as a frontier between the colony and the territory the Mapuche still controlled.

For almost three hundred years, “Chile” in practice meant the central valley. The south was Mapuche land, and the north was desert.

03 Independence, and a Country That Grew North and South

In 1808 Napoleon forced the Spanish king, Ferdinand VII, from his throne, and Spain’s colonies had to decide who governed them. In Santiago a local government junta formed on 18 September 1810, the date Chile still celebrates. After years of war, Chile formally declared its independence in 1818, with Bernardo O’Higgins as its leader, and secured it at the Battle of Maipú that April.

The new republic, like the colony before it, was centred on the middle of the country. It then spent the rest of the century pushing its borders outwards:

  • South: between 1861 and 1883 the Chilean state carried out the military campaign it called the “Pacification of Araucanía”, which ended Mapuche independence and brought the south under Santiago’s control.
  • North: the War of the Pacific (1879–1884) against Peru and Bolivia was fought over the nitrate-rich Atacama Desert. Chile won and took the provinces of Antofagasta and Tarapacá, leaving Bolivia without a coastline.
  • Far south: Chile and Argentina divided Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego in an 1881 treaty that used the Andes as the border, and a British arbitration award in 1902 settled the remaining disputed stretches of the mountains. Chile reached all the way to Cape Horn.

With the Pacific to the west and the Andes to the east, there was nowhere else to grow. The result is the long, thin country on today’s map.

04 One Country, Five Climates

Chile runs from about 17° south to about 56° south, a span of latitude about as wide as the distance from southern Scandinavia to the Sahara. That length gives it an extraordinary range of climates:

It is a natural wonder. It is also a big part of the reason why so much of Chile stays empty.

05 Why the Middle Filled Up

History set the pattern first. Contact with Europeans was catastrophic for Chile’s indigenous peoples: diseases such as smallpox and measles, against which they had no immunity, together with centuries of war, cut their numbers sharply, and the north and south were left thinly populated.

Meanwhile the Spanish built Santiago into the centre of government and farming. In the twentieth century, as Chile industrialised, people from the countryside, and especially from the south, moved to Santiago and the cities around it for work. Over time the capital became the centre of industry, education and government as well.

Today the Santiago metropolitan area has around seven million people, close to 40% of the country. Add the regions of Valparaíso (about 1.9 million), Biobío (about 1.6 million) and Maule (about 1.1 million), plus O’Higgins and Ñuble, and you reach roughly three-quarters of all Chileans, in about 15% of the land.

06 Why the Rest Stayed Empty

Geography did the rest. About 80% of Chile is mountainous, and most of what isn’t is desert, fjord or ice. The central valley is the one large stretch of land that is flat, well watered and mild enough for big cities and farms.

Chile also sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, where the Nazca plate slides beneath South America. Its seismic service records thousands of earthquakes a year, most too small to feel, and the strongest earthquake ever measured, magnitude 9.5, struck Valdivia in 1960. Remote places are harder to reach when disaster strikes: by road, the far-southern region of Magallanes can only be reached from the rest of Chile by driving through Argentina.

And the shape itself is a problem. A country 4,000 km long and a few hundred wide needs very long roads, power lines and supply chains to reach every corner, and every kilometre of them has to cross mountains, rivers or fjords.

07 How Chile Is Trying to Connect Itself

Chile has never stopped trying to link its regions. The clearest example is the Carretera Austral, a road through northern Patagonia begun in 1976. It runs for more than 1,200 km, much of it still unpaved, and parts still depend on ferries across fjords; floods, landslides and dense forest have slowed every stage of its construction.

Newer technology is helping where roads can’t. The Atacama’s sunshine now feeds large solar power plants, internet access reaches the large majority of Chileans, and satellite links serve remote communities. Santiago has built a startup scene around the government’s Start-Up Chile programme, launched in 2010.

The economy rests on what the land offers: Chile is the world’s largest producer of copper, one of the largest producers of lithium, and one of the biggest exporters of farmed salmon. Those industries have helped make it one of the most prosperous and stable countries in South America.

08 Adapting to a Land That Won’t Move

Mountains and oceans cannot be moved, so Chile has chosen to adapt to them. Its story is not only about geography: it is about conquest and resistance, about a state that grew along the only directions open to it, and about people who learned to live with earthquakes, deserts and distance.

Next time you see that thin strip on the world map, remember that it isn’t just a strange shape. It is the result of five centuries of history pressed between an ocean and a mountain range, and of a country that is still working out how to live along all of it.
