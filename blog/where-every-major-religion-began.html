Where Every Major Religion Began

From the Levant to Japan, most of the world’s major religions began in one arc of Asia. Where each one started, who founded it, and how it spread.

Oct 2, 2026 7 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

Look at a map of where the world’s major religions began and something stands out straight away. Almost all of them started in a band of Asia stretching from the eastern Mediterranean to Japan. Judaism, Christianity and Islam began within about 1,500 kilometres of one another. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism all grew up on the Indian subcontinent. China gave rise to Confucianism and Taoism, and Japan to Shinto.

My Short Where Every Major Religion Began covers that map in under a minute. This article goes a little further: where each faith began, roughly when, who is remembered as its founder, and why so many of them started so close together. It describes where religions came from, not which one is right; every tradition here has its own account of its beginnings, and I have tried to give the widely accepted historical picture respectfully.

01 The Levant: Judaism and Christianity

The Levant is the narrow strip of land along the eastern Mediterranean, covering present-day Israel and the Palestinian territories, Lebanon, Syria and Jordan. It sits at the crossroads of Africa, Asia and Europe, and it is where two of the world’s major religions began.

Judaism is one of the oldest monotheistic religions still practised. It developed among the ancient Israelites, and its scriptures, the Hebrew Bible, took shape over many centuries during the first millennium BCE, around the kingdoms of Israel and Judah and the city of Jerusalem. Today there are around 15 million Jews worldwide, most of them in Israel and the United States.

Christianity began in the first century CE with the followers of Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish teacher in Roman-ruled Judea. It spread along the roads and sea lanes of the Roman Empire, and in 380 CE it became the empire’s official religion. It is now the largest religion in the world, with about 2.3 billion followers in 2020 according to the Pew Research Center, around 29% of humanity.

Jerusalem is sacred to Judaism, Christianity and Islam alike, three faiths that trace their roots to the same small region.

02 Arabia: Islam

Just south of the Levant lies the Arabian Peninsula. Islam began there in the early seventh century, in the trading city of Mecca, in present-day Saudi Arabia. Muslims believe the Prophet Muhammad received his first revelation around 610 CE. In 622 he and his followers moved to Medina, a journey known as the Hijra, which marks the first year of the Islamic calendar.

Within a century, Islam had spread from Spain to Central Asia. Today it is the world’s second-largest religion, with about 2 billion followers, and it grew faster than any other major religion between 2010 and 2020. Its centre of gravity has moved east: the country with the largest Muslim population is not in the Middle East at all, but Indonesia.

03 Iran: Zoroastrianism and the Bahá’í Faith

Zoroastrianism is one of the oldest religions in the world still practised. It is based on the teachings of the prophet Zarathustra, known in Greek as Zoroaster, who lived on the Iranian plateau. When he lived is uncertain; scholars’ estimates range from about 1500 to 500 BCE. Zoroastrianism was the official religion of the Sasanian Empire until the Arab conquest of Persia in the seventh century. Today only a small community remains, a few hundred thousand people at most, many of them Parsis in India whose ancestors left Iran more than a thousand years ago.

Much later, Iran was also the birthplace of the Bahá’í Faith. It grew out of the movement of the Báb, who began preaching in Shiraz in 1844, and was founded by Bahá’u’lláh, who announced his mission in Baghdad in 1863 after being exiled from Iran. He spent his final years in the Ottoman province that is now northern Israel, which is why the Bahá’í World Centre is in Haifa and Akko today.

04 The Indian Subcontinent: Four Religions

No region produced more of the world’s major religions than South Asia.

Hinduism has no single founder and no single starting date. It grew over thousands of years from many traditions across ancient South Asia. Its oldest texts, the Vedas, were composed from about 1500 BCE. With about 1.2 billion followers, it is the world’s third-largest religion, and the great majority of Hindus live in India and Nepal.

Jainism also began in ancient India. Jains regard Mahavira, who lived in the sixth or fifth century BCE in what is now the state of Bihar, as the 24th and last of their great teachers, the Tirthankaras. Its central principle is ahimsa, non-violence towards all living beings.

Buddhism began with Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, who was born in Lumbini in present-day Nepal, probably in the fifth century BCE. He is said to have reached enlightenment at Bodh Gaya and preached his first sermon at Sarnath, both in northern India. Buddhism then travelled along trade routes to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan, and today it is far more widespread outside India than inside it.

Sikhism is the youngest of the four. It began in the Punjab region with Guru Nanak, who was born in 1469 in Talwandi, the town now called Nankana Sahib in Pakistan. He was followed by nine more Gurus, and the faith’s holiest site, the Golden Temple, is in Amritsar, India. The Punjab was divided between India and Pakistan in 1947, so the places where Sikhism began now lie in two countries.

05 China: Confucianism and Taoism

Confucianism is based on the teachings of Confucius, who lived from 551 to 479 BCE in the state of Lu, in present-day Shandong province. It focuses on ethics, family duty, education and good government. Whether it counts as a religion or a philosophy is still debated, but for more than two thousand years it shaped how China, Korea, Vietnam and Japan were governed and how families lived.

Taoism, also spelled Daoism, is centred on the Tao, “the Way”, the natural order of the universe. Its classic text, the Tao Te Ching, is traditionally attributed to the sage Laozi and dates from around the fourth century BCE. Organised Taoist religion, with its own priests and temples, emerged later, in the second century CE.

06 Japan: Shinto and Tenrikyo

Shinto is Japan’s native religion. Like Hinduism, it has no founder. It grew out of ancient Japanese reverence for the kami, the spirits found in nature, ancestors and places. Japan’s oldest chronicle, the Kojiki, was completed in 712 CE, and shrines such as Ise remain central to Japanese life today.

Tenrikyo is much younger. It was founded in 1838 by Nakayama Miki, a farmer’s wife in Yamato province, now Nara Prefecture. The city where it began, near Nara, is named Tenri after it.

08 Why Did So Many Religions Begin in Asia?

There is no single answer, but geography explains a good part of it. Some of the world’s earliest cities, kingdoms and systems of writing grew up in three regions of Asia: the Fertile Crescent and the Levant, the river plains of northern India, and the valleys of the Yellow and Yangtze rivers in China. Cities and writing let teachings be recorded, debated and passed down, and growing states and empires carried them further.

Trade did the rest. The Levant and Arabia sat on the routes between the Mediterranean, Africa and the Indian Ocean, and India and China were linked by the Silk Road and by sea. Ideas travelled along those routes with merchants, monks and missionaries, which is how Buddhism reached Japan and Islam reached Indonesia.

That is also why the map of where religions began looks so different from the map of where they are followed today. Most of the world’s Christians now live in Africa, Latin America and Asia rather than in the Middle East or Europe. The largest Muslim population is in Southeast Asia. Buddhism is a minority in India but the main religion of Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka and Cambodia.

09 What This List Leaves Out

“Major” always means making choices. This map follows the religions in the Short, and it leaves out many others: the traditional religions of Africa, the Americas and Oceania, which are among the oldest beliefs on Earth; and newer faiths that began elsewhere, such as Mormonism in the United States and Rastafari in Jamaica. A map of all of them would cover the whole world.

What the map does show is how much of humanity’s religious history began in one great arc of Asia, and how far those ideas have travelled since.
