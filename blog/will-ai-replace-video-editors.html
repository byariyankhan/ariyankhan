Will AI Replace Video Editors? An Honest Answer

I use AI tools most weeks. This isn't a defense of the job or a warning about it — just an honest account of what's actually changed.

Aug 17, 2026 6 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Video Editor & Documentary Creator
Rec 00:00:00:00

I get asked this more than any other question about the job, usually by a client halfway through a call, half-joking, half not: "so how much longer until I don't need you?" I understand why they ask. I'd ask too. But the honest answer isn't the reassuring one people expect, and it isn't the panicked one either. It's just more specific than the question.

01 What I Actually Use AI For

Every project I take on now involves at least one AI tool before I ever open a timeline with intent. Auto-transcription so I can search footage by what was said instead of scrubbing through it. Silence and filler-word detection that flags the dead air I used to hunt for by ear. Noise reduction that used to take a manual pass now handled in the time it takes to make coffee. None of this is a small convenience — it's real hours back on every project, and I'm not precious about admitting that.

This is the part of editing that was always mechanical: repetitive, rule-based, the same decision made the same way every time. It was never the interesting part of the job, and I don't miss doing it by hand.

02 What It Still Can't Do For Me

None of those tools can tell me what a video should say. They can hand me a clean transcript, but they can't tell me which of the forty things a client said in an interview is actually the point — the line the whole video should be built around, buried in minute thirty-one, that the client themselves probably doesn't know is the best thing they said. That call is still entirely mine to make, every time, and it's the one that actually determines whether the finished video works.

It's the same with structure. An AI rough cut can assemble footage in the order it happened. It can't decide that the order it happened in is the wrong order to tell it — that the story actually starts at minute six, or that a detail needs to be withheld for four more minutes to land the way it should. That's judgment, built from having watched a lot of footage and gotten a feel for what an audience needs to know and when.

03 The Question Nobody's Actually Asking

"Will AI replace video editors" treats editing like it's one skill. It isn't — it's the mechanical layer and the judgment layer, bundled together under one job title because for a long time you couldn't buy one without the other. That bundle is coming apart. The real question underneath the panicked one is narrower: what happens to editors whose entire value was the mechanical layer, now that a tool does it faster and for less?

I think that's a real and serious problem for a real segment of the industry — and I don't think it's honest to wave it away with "editors will just adapt." Some won't, not because they're not capable, but because the market for pure technical execution is genuinely shrinking. What isn't shrinking is demand for someone who can look at four hours of unstructured footage and know what to do with it.

The tools got faster at doing what I tell them. They didn't get any better at knowing what to tell them.

04 A Small Example

On a recent documentary project, I ran the raw interview footage through an AI-assisted rough assembly just to see what it would do with it. It found the technically "usable" soundbites — clean audio, complete sentences, no stumbles — and cut them together in the order they were said. Competent, and completely wrong for the video. The strongest moment in the entire interview was a long pause before an answer, followed by a sentence that started, stopped, and restarted differently — exactly the kind of thing a clean-soundbite filter is built to cut around. That pause was the most honest ten seconds in the whole shoot. No tool flagged it, because nothing about it says "usable" on paper. It only reads as usable if you're watching for what a person actually means, not just what they cleanly said.

05 Where I Think This Actually Goes

My honest guess: the mechanical layer keeps getting absorbed, and it should — nobody's craft was ever really in syncing audio or removing background hiss. What gets more valuable, not less, is the editor who can take everything the tools hand back and make a judgment call about it. The job doesn't disappear. It gets smaller in hours and heavier in what those remaining hours are actually worth, which is a very different thing than "video editors are being replaced" — it's closer to the mechanical parts of the job finally getting automated the way they probably should have been all along.

06 What I Actually Tell Clients Who Ask

I tell them the truth: yes, I use AI on every project, and it makes me faster. No, it hasn't made the part of the job they're actually paying me for any less necessary — the read on their footage, the structure, the calls about what stays and what gets cut. If anything, being fast at the mechanical work has left more time for the part that was always the actual craft. That's not a sales pitch. It's just where the job has genuinely landed, at least for now.
