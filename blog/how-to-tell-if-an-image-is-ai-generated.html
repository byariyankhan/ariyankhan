How to Tell If an Image Is AI-Generated: What Actually Works

No single test can prove a picture is real. Here is what each method can tell you, what it cannot, and the order I check them in.

Sep 30, 2026 7 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Video Editor & Documentary Creator
Rec 00:00:00:00

A picture of a flood, a politician, or a product lands in your feed and something about it feels off. Most advice on spotting AI images starts with "look at the hands", which worked in 2023 and works much less now. The more reliable checks are less visual: what the file says about itself, where else the picture has appeared, and whether the scene makes sense. None of them is proof on its own, but together they usually get you to a confident answer.

01 Start With What the File Says About Itself

Every image file can carry hidden text alongside the pixels, called metadata. A photo from a phone normally records the camera model, the lens, the date and often the GPS position. Many AI tools write their own metadata too, and some of it is very direct:

  • Content Credentials (C2PA). A signed record, embedded in the file, of which app created or edited it. Images from ChatGPT and Adobe Firefly, among others, carry one.
  • IPTC "digital source type". A standard photo-industry field. The value trainedAlgorithmicMedia means the image was generated by an AI model.
  • Generator prompts. Stable Diffusion interfaces often save the full prompt, seed and settings as text inside a PNG file.
  • Software names. Plain EXIF and XMP fields sometimes simply name the tool that made the file.

02 Why "No Metadata" Proves Nothing

Here is the catch. Metadata is easy to lose. Taking a screenshot creates a new file with none of the original's information. Most social networks and messaging apps strip or rewrite metadata when you upload, partly for privacy. And anyone who wants to hide an AI origin can remove it in seconds with a tool built for the purpose, including the AI Metadata Remover on this site.

So metadata works in one direction only. If a file says it came from an AI generator, that is strong evidence. If it says nothing, you have learned almost nothing, and you move on to the next check. The same is true of a camera signature: it is encouraging, but EXIF fields can be edited, so it is a clue rather than a guarantee.

A label that says "made with AI" is evidence. The absence of one is just an empty field.

03 Search for the Picture Itself

A reverse image search is often the fastest answer of all. Upload the picture, or paste its address, into Google Lens, Bing Visual Search or TinEye, and look for earlier copies. What you are looking for is the oldest version and where it first appeared.

A genuine news photo usually traces back to a news agency, a photographer or a local account posting from the scene, often in higher resolution than the copy you found. An AI image often traces back to a generator community, an art account, or nowhere at all: it appears suddenly, only in reposts, with no original source. Search results can also turn up fact-checks by news organisations, which saves you the work entirely.

04 The Visual Clues That Still Help

Image generators have improved quickly, and the famous six-fingered hand is now rare. Still, some weaknesses persist, especially in busy scenes and in the parts of the picture that are not the main subject:

  • Text. Signs, labels, licence plates and newspaper headlines that turn into nonsense letters on a closer look.
  • Background people and objects. Faces in a crowd that melt together, railings that merge into walls, chairs with the wrong number of legs.
  • Physics. Shadows falling in different directions, reflections that do not match the scene, water or smoke with no clear source.
  • Surfaces. Skin, fabric and hair with an unnaturally even, polished finish, as if everything were lit by the same soft studio light.
  • Details that repeat. Patterns, windows or bricks that copy each other in ways a real building would not.

Zoom in, and look at the edges of the frame rather than the centre. But treat these as reasons for suspicion, not verdicts. Real photos can have odd reflections and heavy editing, and the best AI images now pass a casual look easily.

05 Check Whether the Scene Makes Sense

Some of the most useful questions have nothing to do with pixels. Does the place look like the place it claims to be? Streets, shop signs, mountains and skylines can be compared with maps and street-level imagery. Is the weather right for the date? Do the uniforms, vehicles and flags match the country? For a claimed news event, did any other photographer or outlet capture the same moment from another angle?

As someone who makes geography videos, this is the check I trust most. A convincing image of an event in a real place has to agree with everything else we know about that place, and AI images often get the general look right while getting the specific geography wrong.

06 What About Invisible Watermarks and AI Detectors?

Some companies now hide a watermark inside the pixels themselves. The best known is Google's SynthID, which is designed to survive cropping, resizing and compression, unlike metadata. It cannot be read by looking at the file's metadata, so tools like the AI Image Checker will not see it; it needs the company's own detector.

Websites that promise to detect any AI image from the pixels alone are a different thing. They can be useful as one more signal, but they make mistakes in both directions: real photos flagged as fake, and generated images passed as real, especially after compression or editing. Never treat a percentage from one of these sites as a final answer, and never accuse someone on the strength of it alone.

07 A Quick Order of Checks

  • Read the metadata. An AI signature or Content Credentials settles it quickly.
  • Run a reverse image search. Find the oldest copy and who posted it first.
  • Look for fact-checks. For news images, someone may have already done the work.
  • Check the scene. Place, date, weather and details against what you can verify.
  • Zoom in on the details. Text, backgrounds, shadows and reflections.
  • Weigh it together. One clue is a hint; several pointing the same way is an answer.

None of this needs special software or skills, only a little patience before sharing. The honest result is sometimes "I can't tell", and that is a perfectly good reason not to pass a picture on.
