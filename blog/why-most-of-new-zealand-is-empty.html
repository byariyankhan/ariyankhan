Why Most of New Zealand Is Still Empty

New Zealand is one of the most liveable countries on Earth, yet most of its people live on one island and a third of them in one city. Here is why.

By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

New Zealand is a remote country in the south-west Pacific, about 2,000 km east of Australia across the Tasman Sea. It is known for towering mountains, clear lakes and green hills, for being the filming location of The Lord of the Rings and The Hobbit, and for being one of the safest and most peaceful countries in the world. Its climate is temperate, much like parts of western Europe, with mild summers and plenty of rain. Yet only about five million people live there, a fraction of the United Kingdom’s population on a similar area of land.

And those people are gathered in very few places. About three-quarters of New Zealanders live on the North Island, and roughly one in three lives in a single city, Auckland. Most of the land, especially on the South Island, is mountains, farmland or protected wilderness, with very few people at all. Why, in a country so beautiful and so liveable, is so much of it still empty?

01 The Most Remote Rich Country on Earth

The first reason is distance. Look at a world map and New Zealand is far from every major landmass except Australia, and even Australia is a three-hour flight away. From Europe, Asia or the Americas a trip takes the better part of a day in the air. It was one of the last large areas of habitable land on Earth that humans reached.

02 The Māori: First to Arrive

The first people to settle New Zealand were the ancestors of the Māori, Polynesian navigators who crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean in double-hulled canoes. Archaeology dates their arrival to around the late 13th century.

Māori tradition tells the story of Kupe, a great navigator from the Polynesian homeland of Hawaiki. In one well-known version, a giant octopus kept stealing the bait and catch of his people, and Kupe chased it across the ocean until he reached a new land, which he named Aotearoa, often translated as “land of the long white cloud”. He then returned to Hawaiki with directions for others to follow.

The islands they found had no land mammals except bats. Instead there were forests, rivers full of fish, rich coastlines and enormous flightless birds, the moa, which the settlers hunted. The Māori built a rich culture around this new land: stories, carving, song and dance, and a belief in spiritual guardians of nature such as Tāne, the god of forests. Community life centred on the marae, the meeting ground where people gathered to eat, speak and make decisions together.

03 Europeans, a Treaty and a War

For centuries New Zealand was untouched by the outside world. That changed in 1642, when the Dutch explorer Abel Tasman arrived on the coast of the South Island; a violent encounter with Māori left four of his crew dead, and he sailed away without landing. In 1769 the British navigator James Cook arrived and circumnavigated both main islands, and his encounters with Māori also turned deadly. But Cook’s detailed charts opened the way for European whalers, sealers, traders and missionaries.

In 1840 many Māori chiefs and representatives of the British Crown signed the Treaty of Waitangi, which made New Zealand a British colony. The English and Māori texts of the treaty differ in important ways, and what exactly was agreed is still debated today.

The following decades were devastating for Māori. European diseases such as measles and influenza, against which they had no immunity, spread quickly, and disputes over land led to the New Zealand Wars, fought between the 1840s and the early 1870s, followed by large confiscations of Māori land. By the 1890s the Māori population had fallen to around 42,000, far below what it had been when Cook arrived.

The New Zealand Wars were not a single war but decades of conflict over one question: who would own and control the land.

04 A Culture That Came Back

Māori gained four seats in Parliament in 1867, and in the twentieth century their population recovered strongly. Today Māori culture is central to New Zealand’s identity. The Māori language, te reo Māori, is an official language, Māori art and carving are admired around the world, and the haka is known everywhere, not least because the national rugby team, the All Blacks, performs it before every match.

05 Why the Land Stays Empty

History explains why New Zealand was settled late. Geography explains where people live now.

  • The Southern Alps run almost the length of the South Island, with peaks above 3,700 metres. There is little flat land between them and the sea on the wet west coast, and much of the mountain country is too steep and too rugged for towns or farms.
  • The rain shadow. Westerly winds drop most of their rain on the mountains, leaving the eastern side of the South Island much drier. The Canterbury Plains can be farmed, but much of the dry inland is sheep country with very few people.
  • Protected land. About a third of New Zealand is set aside as national parks and conservation land, where no one can build at all.

The North Island is warmer, gentler and closer to the rest of the world, so most people live there. Auckland, with its harbours and its role as the country’s gateway, has grown into a city of about 1.7 million.

06 Immigration and the Auckland Problem

After the Second World War, New Zealand encouraged immigration to grow its small population, first mainly from Britain and then from the Pacific Islands, Asia and around the world. Today more than a quarter of New Zealand’s people were born overseas, one of the highest shares of any country.

Most newcomers settle in Auckland, which has pushed its housing costs among the highest in the world relative to incomes. Governments have tried to encourage growth in other cities and regions so that the rest of the country can share in it.

Life in New Zealand still mixes adventure and calm: hiking, skiing and surfing are never far away, cities like Auckland and Wellington have busy café and festival scenes, and New Zealanders, or Kiwis, are known for their relaxed and friendly attitude.

07 An Economy Built on Grass

New Zealand’s green hills and heavy rain make it ideal for grazing. The country has around five million people and roughly 24 million sheep, along with millions of dairy and beef cattle. Farming is the backbone of its exports: dairy products are the single largest export, followed by meat, and together primary industries earn tens of billions of New Zealand dollars a year.

The same landscape powers the country. More than 80% of New Zealand’s electricity comes from renewable sources, mainly hydropower, with geothermal and wind adding more every year. Conservation is taken seriously too, from the national parks to long-running efforts to protect the kiwi and other native birds from introduced predators.

08 Small, Remote and Still Inspiring

New Zealand’s emptiness is not a failure. It is the result of isolation, rugged mountains and a painful colonial history, and also of a choice to keep much of the land wild. Its story shows how a small, remote country can balance growth with protection, and modern life with a culture that almost disappeared and came back.

Next time you look at those two islands at the edge of the world map, remember that the empty spaces are part of the story, not a gap in it.
