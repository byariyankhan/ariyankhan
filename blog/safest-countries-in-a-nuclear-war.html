The Safest Countries in a Nuclear War: New Zealand, Iceland and Switzerland

Two remote islands that could feed themselves, and a country in the middle of Europe with a shelter for everyone. What each one has going for it, and where the popular story goes too far.

Oct 3, 2026 6 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

If a world war ever turned nuclear, no country would be untouched. Missiles would fall mostly on the Northern Hemisphere’s great powers, but smoke from burning cities could cool the whole planet, ruin harvests and disrupt trade everywhere. So the real question is not where you could escape a nuclear war, but where people would have the best chance of getting through the years that followed.

My video Safest Countries During WW3 picks three answers: New Zealand, Iceland and Switzerland. They are safe for very different reasons. Two are remote islands that could probably keep feeding themselves; the third sits in the middle of Europe but has spent sixty years building shelters for its entire population. Here is what each one has going for it, and where the popular story goes further than the facts.

01 What “Safe” Means in a Nuclear War

Three things decide how a country would fare. The first is distance: how far it is from likely targets and from the countries most likely to fight. The second is fallout and climate: whether radioactive dust and the soot of burning cities would reach it, and how badly its harvests would suffer. The third is self-reliance: whether it could feed its people, keep the lights on and stay orderly with world trade cut off.

The climate effect is the one most people miss. A 2022 study in the journal Nature Food modelled how soot from burning cities would block sunlight and cut crop yields. In its largest scenario, a full war between the United States and Russia, it estimated that more than five billion people could die of famine within two years. That is why food matters as much as bunkers.

Surviving the blast is the short part of the problem. Feeding a country for years without sunlight or trade is the long part.

02 Number Three: New Zealand

New Zealand is one of the most isolated developed countries on Earth. It lies in the South Pacific, more than 2,000 kilometres from Australia and much further from any nuclear power. Most of the world’s nuclear weapons, and most of its likely targets, are in the Northern Hemisphere, and the air of the two hemispheres mixes only slowly, which would limit how much fallout reached the south.

It also has a strong case on food. A 2023 study by New Zealand researchers Matt Boyd and Nick Wilson, published in Risk Analysis, looked at which island nations could best keep feeding their people in a “nuclear winter”. Australia and New Zealand came out on top, followed by Iceland, the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu. New Zealand produces far more food than its five million people eat, and its mild, ocean-moderated climate would soften the cooling.

Its reputation has drawn attention from the very rich. The tech investor Peter Thiel became a New Zealand citizen in 2011 and bought land near Lake Wānaka, and reports of wealthy foreigners buying remote properties and installing bunkers have become a running story. New Zealand has also been officially nuclear-free since 1987, which makes it an unlikely target in its own right.

Its weak point is fuel. New Zealand closed its only oil refinery, at Marsden Point, in 2022 and now imports all its refined fuel. Without diesel, tractors, fishing boats and lorries stop, so the same researchers have urged the country to plan for keeping farms and transport running if imports were cut off.

03 Number Two: Iceland

Iceland’s name is misleading. Its interior is glaciers, lava fields and highlands, but its coasts and lowlands are green, farmed and comfortable to live in. About 390,000 people live there, in the middle of the North Atlantic, far from the main European and Asian powers.

It has some unusual strengths. Iceland has topped the Global Peace Index, a yearly ranking of how peaceful countries are, every year since 2008. It has no army of its own, only a coast guard. And almost all of its electricity comes from hydroelectric and geothermal power, so it does not depend on imported oil or gas to keep the lights on and its homes warm. Geothermal heat even lets it grow vegetables in greenhouses year-round, and its fishing grounds are among the richest in the world.

The picture has two caveats. Iceland is a founding member of NATO and hosts allied military activity at Keflavík, so it is not entirely outside a war between the great powers. And it imports much of its grain and other food, which it would have to replace with fish, meat, dairy and what greenhouses can grow. It appears in the 2023 island-refuge study for good reason, but behind Australia and New Zealand.

04 Number One: Switzerland

Switzerland is the opposite case. It is not remote at all: it is landlocked in the middle of Western Europe. What makes it stand out is preparation. Since the 1960s, Swiss law has aimed to give every resident a place in a nuclear shelter, and the country has built more protected space per person than anywhere else in the world.

According to Switzerland’s Federal Office for Civil Protection, there are about 370,000 shelters in homes, institutions and hospitals, plus a few thousand public shelters, providing roughly nine million protected places, slightly more than the population. Figures quoted vary from year to year, and the video uses 384,000. Many new buildings must include a shelter, or their owners pay into a fund that builds public ones instead. The shelters are built to withstand a nearby blast, have ventilation systems with filters against radioactive dust and chemical agents, and are inspected regularly.

Two parts of the popular story need correcting. Switzerland is not “officially” the safest country in the world; no such official ranking exists. And the shelters are not kept stocked with food. Many private shelters are used as cellars and storerooms in peacetime, and must be cleared and made ready within days if the authorities order it. Food comes from elsewhere: households are asked to keep an emergency supply at home, and the country holds compulsory national stockpiles of food, fuel and medicines for crises.

Its location cuts both ways. A neutral country with no nuclear weapons is an unlikely target, but it is surrounded by NATO members, and fallout and a cooling climate would not stop at its borders. Shelters protect people for days or weeks, not from years of poor harvests.

06 Other Countries Often Named

These three are not the only answers. Australia ranks alongside New Zealand in the food studies, though it has far more strategic targets, including bases used by the United States. Argentina, Uruguay and other Southern Hemisphere food exporters often appear on such lists too. And Finland has its own extensive shelter network, with room for most of its population.

07 Where Would You Go?

Each country answers a different part of the problem. New Zealand and Iceland are about distance and the ability to keep living without the rest of the world; Switzerland is about protecting people in the first dangerous weeks. None of them offers real safety from a nuclear war, and the best protection of all is that one never happens.
