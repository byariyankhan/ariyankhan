Israel’s Geography: Why a Small Country Has Always Felt Surrounded

A narrow strip of land where three continents meet and three faiths hold their holiest places. How geography shaped Israel’s wars, its strategy and the conflict with the Palestinians.

Sep 30, 2026 7 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

Israel is one of the most developed countries in the Middle East, and one of the smallest: about 22,000 square kilometres, less than a tenth of the size of the United Kingdom. Its geography has shaped almost everything about it. It borders Egypt to the south-west, Jordan to the east, and Syria and Lebanon to the north, and it controls or surrounds the two Palestinian territories, the West Bank and the Gaza Strip. At different times it has fought wars with every one of its neighbours, and states further away, above all Iran, have treated it as an enemy.

Why has so much conflict gathered around such a small piece of land? Two reasons stand out: where it is, and what it means to three of the world’s great religions. This article looks at how geography has shaped Israel’s security, its wars and its long conflict with the Palestinians.

01 A Land Bridge Between Continents

Israel lies in the Levant, the strip of land along the eastern Mediterranean between the sea and the deserts of Arabia and Mesopotamia. It is the only land route between Africa and Asia, and for thousands of years armies, traders and empires have passed through it. Whoever wanted to move between Egypt and Mesopotamia, or later between Europe and the Indian Ocean, had to cross or control this corridor.

That made the region valuable and exposed. Small states here rarely stayed independent for long when larger powers rose around them.

02 A Holy Land for Three Faiths

The second reason is faith. Jerusalem is sacred to Jews, Christians and Muslims:

  • For Jews, the Temple Mount is the site of the ancient Temples, the holiest place in Judaism.
  • For Christians, Jerusalem is where Jesus was crucified and, they believe, rose again; the Church of the Holy Sepulchre marks the site.
  • For Muslims, the same hilltop, known as the Noble Sanctuary, holds the Al-Aqsa Mosque and the Dome of the Rock, making it the third holiest site in Islam.

Because the most sacred ground of three religions overlaps on a few acres, control of Jerusalem has never been only a question of borders.

03 Three Thousand Years of Changing Rulers

Around 600 BC, Jerusalem was the capital of the Kingdom of Judah, squeezed between the great powers of Egypt and Babylon. In 586 BC the Babylonians destroyed the city and its First Temple. The Persian Empire conquered Babylon a few decades later and allowed exiles to return, and after Alexander the Great the region passed to Hellenistic kingdoms and then to Rome, which destroyed the Second Temple in AD 70.

Jesus lived and was crucified under Roman rule, which is why Jerusalem became central to Christianity. In 637 Muslim armies took the city, and over the following decades the Dome of the Rock and the Al-Aqsa Mosque were built on the Temple Mount. Apart from the Crusader period, the region was ruled by Muslim states for most of the next 1,300 years, finally by the Ottoman Empire.

04 1948: One State Born, Another People Displaced

After the First World War the Ottoman Empire collapsed and Britain governed the land as the Mandate of Palestine. Tension grew between its Arab population and Jewish immigrants arriving in large numbers, many fleeing persecution in Europe. In 1947 the United Nations proposed dividing the territory into a Jewish state and an Arab state. Jewish leaders accepted the plan; Arab leaders rejected it.

On 14 May 1948, as the British left, Jewish leaders declared the State of Israel. The next day the armies of Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq attacked. Israel won the war and ended it holding more territory than the UN plan had given it. For Palestinians, 1948 is remembered as the Nakba, the “catastrophe”: around 700,000 people fled or were driven from their homes, and most were never allowed to return. Both memories still shape the conflict today.

05 The Wars That Followed

More wars came: the Suez crisis of 1956, the Six-Day War of 1967, the War of Attrition along the Suez Canal from 1967 to 1970, and the Yom Kippur War of 1973, when Egypt and Syria launched a surprise attack. The 1967 war changed the map most: Israel took the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, Gaza and the Sinai Peninsula from Egypt, and the Golan Heights from Syria.

Israel’s military strategy grew directly out of its geography. With no room to retreat and most of its population close to its borders, it relied on striking first when it saw an attack coming, fighting on the enemy’s territory rather than its own, and ending wars quickly before international pressure or sheer numbers could turn them.

The map of enemies has also changed. Egypt signed a peace treaty with Israel in 1979 and got the Sinai back; Jordan made peace in 1994; and in 2020 the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain and Morocco normalised relations with Israel in the Abraham Accords. Israel today is no longer surrounded by hostile states on every side, but by a mix of cold peace, open hostility and unresolved conflict.

06 Natural Barriers, and Their Limits

Unlike a country such as the United States, protected by two oceans, Israel has few strong natural defences:

  • The Sinai Peninsula, a vast, thinly populated desert under Egyptian control, lies between Israel and Egypt’s heartland. Since the 1979 treaty much of it has been demilitarised.
  • The Negev Desert covers more than half of Israel’s territory and stretches to the Red Sea at Eilat.
  • The Jordan Valley and the deserts beyond it lie to the east, towards Jordan.

The weak point is the country’s narrow waist. Before 1967, Israel’s coastal plain, where most of its people live, was only about 15 kilometres wide at its narrowest, between the sea and the hills of the West Bank. That is a big part of why Israeli governments have seen control of those hills as a security question, and why Palestinians see the same land as the heart of their future state.

07 The West Bank and the Oslo Map

The Palestinian territories are split in two with no land link between them: the West Bank and the Gaza Strip. After 1967 Israel began building Jewish settlements in the West Bank. Most of the international community considers these settlements illegal under international law; Israel disputes that.

In 1993 Israeli and Palestinian leaders signed the first Oslo Accord, negotiated in secret in Norway and signed in Washington. A second agreement in 1995 divided the West Bank into three zones:

08 Gaza, the Barrier and Jerusalem

The Gaza Strip is one of the most densely populated places on Earth, about two million people in an area roughly 40 kilometres long. Israel withdrew its settlers and soldiers in 2005; Hamas took control in 2007, and Israel and Egypt then tightly restricted the movement of people and goods. Israel says the restrictions stop weapons reaching Hamas; UN officials and human rights groups have described life there as being inside an open-air prison. Since Hamas’s attack on Israel on 7 October 2023 and the war that followed, Gaza has faced a humanitarian catastrophe, with most of its people displaced.

In 2002, during the Second Intifada, Israel began building a separation barrier between itself and the West Bank. Israel says it stopped suicide bombings; Palestinians say its route, much of it inside the West Bank, takes their land, and in 2004 the International Court of Justice gave an advisory opinion that those sections were contrary to international law.

Jerusalem remains the most contested city of all. Israel controls all of it and considers it its undivided capital; Palestinians seek East Jerusalem as the capital of a future state, and most countries do not recognise Israel’s annexation of the eastern half.

09 Geography, History and Faith

The conflict no longer belongs only to the Middle East. The United States, Europe, Russia and the region’s own powers all have interests there, from religion to energy to strategy. And yet, despite its small size and lack of strategic depth, Israel has not only survived but become one of the strongest military and technological powers in its region, while the question of a Palestinian state remains unanswered.

Next time you look at this narrow strip on the map, remember that it is where three continents meet, where three faiths hold their holiest places, and where two peoples claim the same land.
