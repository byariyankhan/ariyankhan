How Much Does a Video Editor Cost?

"It depends" is the honest answer. Here's everything it actually depends on, so you can figure out what you should be paying.

Aug 16, 2026 5 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Video Editor & Documentary Creator
Rec 00:00:00:00

Every creator asks this before they hire anyone, and every editor gives some version of the same unsatisfying answer: it depends. That's not evasive — it's genuinely true. A 10-minute YouTube video with clean audio and one camera angle costs a fraction of what a 40-minute podcast episode with three cameras, heavy b-roll, and same-day turnaround costs. Below is what actually moves the number, so "it depends" stops being a dodge and starts being something you can plan around.

01 The Three Common Pricing Models

Most editors price their work one of three ways, and each one suits a different kind of client:

  • Per video. A flat rate for one finished video, regardless of exactly how long editing takes. Common for creators who publish occasionally or want budget certainty per upload.
  • Per minute of raw footage. The rate scales with how much footage the editor has to sit through, not just how long the final video is. This is common when raw footage length varies a lot between projects.
  • Monthly retainer. A fixed monthly rate for a set number of videos, usually with a discount versus paying per video individually. This is the model most weekly YouTube channels and podcasts end up on once the relationship is established.

None of these is objectively "correct" — they're just different ways of splitting the same underlying cost, which is the editor's time.

02 What Actually Drives the Price Up

Two videos of the same length can cost very different amounts. The length of the final video matters far less than these factors:

  • Raw footage volume. An hour of raw footage cut down to 8 minutes takes longer than an 8-minute video shot in one clean take.
  • Audio quality going in. Clean, well-recorded audio is fast to edit. Noisy rooms, inconsistent mic levels, or multiple speakers on one track add real time to cleanup.
  • Captions and graphics. Auto-generated captions are nearly free. Hand-styled animated captions, lower thirds, and callouts take meaningfully longer.
  • B-roll and cutaways. Sourcing, syncing, and placing b-roll well — especially screen recordings or stock footage matched to specific lines — is one of the slowest parts of the edit.
  • Color grading and sound design. A basic color correction pass is quick. A full grade with consistent look across multiple cameras, plus custom sound design, is a different level of work.
  • Turnaround time. A rush request compresses everything into less time, which usually carries a premium — the same way it would with any service.
  • Revision rounds. Unlimited revisions built into a price cost more than a package with one or two defined rounds, because they're an open-ended time commitment for the editor.

You're not paying for minutes of video. You're paying for hours of someone's attention.

03 Typical Price Ranges You'll See

These are broad, general ranges based on what's common across the freelance market — not a quote, and not specific to any one editor's pricing. Treat them as a starting orientation, not a rulebook:

  • New or budget editors: often the cheapest option, and sometimes genuinely good value — but portfolios and consistency vary widely at this tier, so review samples carefully.
  • Experienced freelance editors with a track record and a specific niche (talking head, documentary, short-form) usually charge a mid-range rate that reflects both skill and reliability.
  • Agencies and editing teams tend to sit at the top of the range, since you're paying for account management, redundancy, and guaranteed capacity, not just the edit itself.

Where a specific editor lands inside these tiers depends on demand, specialization, and how much of the process — story notes, thumbnails, publishing — they take on beyond the raw edit.

04 What You Should Get at Each Price Point

Price alone doesn't tell you what's included. Before comparing numbers, confirm what's actually in the package:

  • Basic tier: clean cuts, dead air removed, basic audio leveling, auto-generated captions. Good for simple, low-stakes content.
  • Mid tier: everything above, plus b-roll placement, styled captions, color correction, and at least one revision round. This is where most established YouTube channels sit.
  • Premium tier: everything above, plus custom motion graphics, full color grading, sound design, and closer collaboration on pacing and structure — closer to a creative partner than a hired hand.

05 Red Flags in Pricing

A low price isn't automatically a red flag — plenty of skilled editors charge less while they build a portfolio. What's worth being cautious about is a mismatch between the price and the promise:

  • A rate far below the going range paired with claims of unlimited revisions and same-day turnaround — something in that combination usually gives.
  • No willingness to explain what's included at that price, or a quote given before they've seen any of your footage.
  • Pricing that changes significantly after you've already committed to the raw footage handoff.

06 Questions to Ask Before You Get a Quote

A good quote starts with the editor asking you questions, not the other way around. But if they don't, come prepared with these:

  • What's included in this price — captions, b-roll, color, sound?
  • How many revision rounds are built in, and what happens beyond that?
  • What's the realistic turnaround for a video this length and complexity?
  • Do you charge per video, per minute of raw footage, or on retainer?
  • What happens if my raw footage turns out to be messier than expected?

Editors who answer these clearly and specifically, without hedging, are usually the ones who've done this enough times to know exactly where their own time goes.

07 How I Approach Pricing

I don't publish a flat rate, for the same reason nobody else honestly can — a clean 10-minute solo talking head video and a rough 40-minute multi-camera podcast aren't the same job. What I can tell you is what happens before a number gets attached to anything: I look at your actual footage, ask about captions, b-roll, and turnaround expectations, and give a real quote based on what your specific video needs — not a generic package that may include work you don't need or leave out work you do.
