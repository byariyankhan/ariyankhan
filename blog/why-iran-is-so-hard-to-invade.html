Why Iran Is So Hard to Invade

Rings of mountains, two great deserts and the world’s most important oil strait. How geography turned Iran into a fortress, and what it has built on top of it.

Sep 30, 2026 5 MIN READ
By Ariyan Khan Documentary Creator & Video Editor
Rec 00:00:00:00

Iran is one of the oldest civilisations in the world, and one of the hardest countries on Earth to invade. Many great powers have tried to dominate it: Alexander’s Macedonians, Arab armies, Mongols, Turks, the Russian and British empires, and in the modern era the United States. Some of them conquered it for a time, and in 1941 British and Soviet forces occupied it during the Second World War. None of them erased it, and when Iraq tried a full invasion in 1980, it failed. A big part of the reason is geography.

This article looks at the mountains, deserts and seas that make Iran a natural fortress, the war that proved it, and the strategy Iran has built on top of its geography since 1979.

01 At the Crossroads of Regions

Iran sits where the Middle East, Central Asia, South Asia and the Caucasus meet. It borders seven countries: Pakistan, Afghanistan and Turkmenistan to the east, and Iraq, Turkey, Armenia and Azerbaijan to the west. It also touches three bodies of water: the Caspian Sea in the north, and the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman in the south.

A state has existed in roughly today’s shape since the Safavid dynasty united the country in 1501, although Iran lost territory in the Caucasus to Russia and in the east to what became Afghanistan during the 1800s.

02 The Strait of Hormuz

Between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman lies one of the most important waterways in the world, the Strait of Hormuz. At its narrowest it is about 39 kilometres wide, yet around a fifth of the world’s oil and a large share of its liquefied natural gas pass through it.

Near the strait, Iran has controlled three small islands, Abu Musa and the Greater and Lesser Tunbs, since 1971. The United Arab Emirates also claims them, and the dispute remains unresolved. Iran’s main southern port, Bandar Abbas, sits right beside the strait, and its coastline on the Gulf and the Gulf of Oman runs for more than 2,400 kilometres.

03 A Natural Fortress

Look at a relief map and Iran is a high plateau ringed by mountains and filled with deserts.

An invader who crossed the coast would still face the mountains, and beyond them the deserts. Moving and supplying an army across that terrain is extremely hard.

04 The War That Proved It: Iran–Iraq, 1980–1988

In September 1980, Iraq under Saddam Hussein invaded Iran. The immediate dispute was the Shatt al-Arab, the waterway at the head of the Gulf that carries Iraq’s oil exports; Saddam tore up a 1975 border agreement and wanted full control of it. He also aimed to seize Iran’s oil-rich Khuzestan province and to weaken the new Islamic Republic, born in the revolution of 1979.

Several Gulf monarchies, among them Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, feared that Iran’s revolution would spread to their own countries, and they backed Iraq financially; Iraq also received weapons and support from a range of foreign powers.

Iraq captured parts of Khuzestan, but it never broke into the Iranian heartland: the Zagros and the sheer depth of the country stopped it. After eight years the war ended in a UN-brokered ceasefire in 1988, with no change to the border. It was one of the bloodiest conflicts of the twentieth century, with hundreds of thousands killed and many more wounded, and by some estimates its economic cost to the two countries passed a trillion dollars.

Iraq won battles on the plain. It could not win in the mountains.

05 What the Land Holds

Iran’s geography is also rich. It holds around a tenth of the world’s proven oil reserves and roughly a sixth of its natural gas, most of it near the Persian Gulf, along with large deposits of copper, zinc and other metals in its mountains. Those resources make Iran important to the world economy, and they are one more reason great powers have always paid attention to it.

06 From Western Ally to Islamic Republic

It was not always opposed to the West. Before 1979, under the Shah, Iran was one of the United States’ closest allies in the region; it bought American fighter jets and was a pillar of US strategy in the Gulf. It even had working relations with Israel: in 1950 Iran became the second Muslim-majority country, after Turkey, to recognise Israel, in practice if not formally.

The revolution of 1979 changed everything. Ayatollah Khomeini’s government cut ties with Israel, handed the Israeli mission in Tehran to the Palestine Liberation Organization, and turned against the United States. Iran’s neighbours in the Gulf, fearing the revolution would spread, grew hostile too, and the Iran–Iraq War began the following year.

07 The Network Beyond Its Borders

Isolated and with hostile states between it and its main rival, Israel, Iran built influence beyond its borders by backing allied armed groups. Iran calls this network the “Axis of Resistance”. It has included Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas and other groups in Gaza, militias in Iraq such as the Badr Organization, fighters sent to support the Assad government in Syria, and the Houthi movement in Yemen. The United States and many other countries designate several of these groups as terrorist organisations.

The network lets Iran put pressure on its rivals from far away, without fighting them directly, and Iranian-designed drones and missiles have spread through it. It has also suffered heavy blows: Hezbollah was badly weakened by Israel in 2024, and the Assad government in Syria fell in December of that year.

In June 2025, Israel and Iran fought a twelve-day war. Israel struck Iranian nuclear and military sites, the United States bombed three nuclear facilities, and Iran answered with waves of missiles and drones against Israel. It ended in a ceasefire, with both sides claiming success.

08 A Fortress With Open Questions

Iran’s mountains, deserts and long coastline make a land invasion almost unthinkable, and its control over the Strait of Hormuz gives it a lever over the world’s energy supply. Its missile and nuclear programmes, its regional allies and its ties with Russia and China make it one of the central powers of the Middle East.

The open question is how long geography alone can carry it. Sanctions, a weakened network of allies, pressure from Israel and the United States, and tensions at home will all test the country in the years ahead. Next time you look at Iran on a map, notice the rings of mountains around its plateau: they explain much of its past, but not yet its future.
