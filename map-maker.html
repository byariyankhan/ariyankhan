How It Works

A custom map in under a minute

  1. Pick your countriesClick them on the map or type a name (aliases like “UK”, “USA” or “Burma” work). Add more colour groups to compare, say, NATO members against partners, or the countries a trade route passes through.
  2. Frame the shotChoose a projection, a region preset or “Zoom to selection”, then set the canvas to 16:9 for a video, 9:16 for a Short, or 1:1 for a post. Use the zoom and margin sliders to leave room for your titles.
  3. ExportDownload a PNG at 2× (4K) or 4× (8K), or an SVG you can recolour and animate in After Effects, Illustrator or Figma. Tick “transparent” to drop the map straight over your footage.

Thumbnail tip: highlight one country in yellow on a dark map, zoom in until it fills a third of the frame, and put your text in the empty ocean side. High contrast, one focal point, readable at 200 pixels wide. That is the thumbnail formula that keeps working for geography and history channels.

Projections

Which projection should you use?

Every flat map lies about something. Pick the lie that matters least for your story.

ProjectionBest forWatch out
Natural EarthWhole-world videos and thumbnails. Modern, balanced, the default here.Slight shape distortion near the poles.
Equal EarthWhen relative country size is the point (population, emissions, land area).Looks a little stretched at the edges.
RobinsonThe classic “atlas” look; familiar to older audiences and textbooks.Neither equal-area nor conformal, a compromise on both.
Winkel TripelNational Geographic's choice; very low overall distortion.Curved edges leave odd corners on a 16:9 canvas.
MercatorZoomed-in regions, navigation, matching Google Maps screenshots.Greenland looks as big as Africa. Never use it for a world map about size.
MillerA Mercator that tolerates the poles; fills a rectangle neatly.Same size lies, only slightly smaller.
EquirectangularOverlaying data grids, or when you need lat/long to be straight lines.Flat and plain; countries near the equator look squashed.
Globe (Orthographic)A single country or continent reveal; cinematic, works great on 9:16.Only half the world is visible at once, by design.
Why This Exists

I make map animations for a living

Most of my editing work is for geography, history, economics and geopolitics channels, and almost every project starts the same way: a still map with the right countries highlighted, in the right projection, at the right aspect ratio. Clients would send me screenshots of Google Maps, Wikipedia SVGs with the wrong colours, or a paint-bucket job in PowerPoint. Getting a clean base map used to cost twenty minutes per shot.

So I built the tool I wanted: public-domain Natural Earth borders, proper projections, a globe, colour groups, and an export that lands directly in the timeline. It runs entirely in your browser, nothing is uploaded, and it is free because a good base map is the least interesting part of a great map video.

What the tool does not do

  • No animation. It makes stills. Reveals, zooms, route lines and camera moves are done in After Effects, which is the service this page sits next to.
  • No city points or custom shapes yet. Countries and territories only, from Natural Earth. If you need provinces or cities, export the SVG and add them in Illustrator or Figma.
  • No political judgement. Borders follow Natural Earth's de facto policy. Add your own caption where your audience expects one.

Natural Earth or Equal Earth for a whole-world view: they keep country sizes honest and look modern. Mercator makes Greenland look bigger than Africa, so avoid it for world maps, but it is fine when you zoom into a single region. The Globe projection is the most cinematic for a single country or continent reveal.

The default dataset is Natural Earth 1:110m, which leaves out micro-states such as Singapore, Malta, Bahrain and most island territories to keep the page fast. Tick “High-detail borders” to load the 1:50m dataset with 241 countries and territories, including all of those.

Borders follow Natural Earth's de facto policy: the map shows who controls an area today, not who claims it. That means Kashmir, Western Sahara, Taiwan, Kosovo, Somaliland, Northern Cyprus, Crimea and similar areas are drawn one particular way that some viewers will disagree with. The map is not a political statement, and you should add your own caption or disclaimer where it matters for your audience.
