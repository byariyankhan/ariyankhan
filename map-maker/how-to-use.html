How to use Map Maker

Everything the tool can do, in the order you will need it, plus five recipes for the maps people make most often.

1. Choose countries

There are two ways to add a country, and you can mix them:

  • Click it on the map. Hovering shows the country’s name, so you can check before you click. Click a highlighted country again to take it off.
  • Search for it. Type in the search box under Countries. The list narrows as you type; press Enter to add the first match, or click any name in the list. Common alternative names are understood: “UK”, “Britain” and “England” find the United Kingdom, “USA” and “America” the United States, “Burma” finds Myanmar, “Holland” the Netherlands, “Czech Republic” Czechia, “Ivory Coast” Côte d’Ivoire, “DRC” or “Zaire” the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and “Türkiye” Turkey.

Every country you add appears as a small chip under its group. Press the × on a chip to remove that country, or Clear all under the map to start again.

Small countries missing? The default borders (Natural Earth 1:110m) leave out micro-states such as Singapore, Malta and Bahrain to keep the page fast. Tick High-detail borders under View to load the 1:50m set: 241 countries and territories, about 700 KB.

2. Colour groups

A group is a set of countries that share one colour and one label. You start with one group called Highlighted; + Add colour group adds another, up to six.

  • The active group has a blue outline. New countries go into it. Click a group’s colour square to make it active.
  • Rename a group by typing in its name field. With Labels switched on, the name is what appears on the map.
  • Change its colour with the colour picker next to the name. The count beside it shows how many countries the group holds.
  • Delete a group with its ×; its countries go back to plain land. There is always at least one group, so the last one has no ×.

Groups are what turn a locator map into an explanation: founding members against later members, exporters against importers, the countries a pipeline crosses against the ones it avoids.

3. Projection, region and canvas

The View panel decides what part of the world is in the frame and at what shape.

  • Projection: eight choices, from Natural Earth (the default) to a 3D-looking globe. See which one to use below.
  • Region: jump straight to Europe, Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, Africa, North America, the Caribbean & Central America, South America or Oceania.
  • Zoom to selection (under the map) fits the frame around the countries you have highlighted. Whole world goes back to the full view.
  • Canvas sets the shape and base size of the image. The export sizes are in the table in section 5.
  • Zoom (0.5× to 6×) and Margin fine-tune the framing. Leave a wide margin if you need empty ocean for a title.

4. Style

Start from a preset and adjust from there:

  • Dark: charcoal land on near-black, the classic YouTube explainer look.
  • Light: grey land on white, for documents and slides.
  • Atlas: sand land on a blue ocean, like a school atlas.
  • Slate: blue-grey, calmer than Dark.
  • Paper: cream and brown ink; prints well.

Then set your own land, ocean and border colours and border width. Tick Transparent ocean/background to export only the land. Globe/sphere outline draws the edge of the world, Grid lines adds lines of latitude and longitude, and Labels on highlighted countries writes each group’s name, with its own colour and a size from 10 to 72.

5. Export PNG or SVG

Choose a PNG size and press Download PNG, or Download SVG for a vector file. Copy PNG to clipboard puts the image straight into Slack, Docs or an image editor, in browsers that allow it; if yours doesn’t, the tool says so and you can download instead. Everything is drawn on your own device.

6. Share a map by link

Copy share link copies the page address with the whole map encoded after the #: countries, groups, colours, projection, canvas and style. Nothing is saved on a server. Whoever opens the link sees exactly your map and can keep editing it, which makes it a good way to hand a map to an editor or a colleague. The address in your browser updates as you edit, so bookmarking the page also keeps the map for later.

7. Which projection to use

Every flat map distorts something. Pick the distortion that matters least for your story.

ProjectionBest forWatch out
Natural EarthWhole-world videos and thumbnails. Balanced and modern; the default.Slight distortion of shapes near the poles.
Equal EarthWhen relative size is the point: population, emissions, land area.Looks a little stretched at the edges.
RobinsonThe familiar textbook-atlas look.A compromise: neither equal-area nor true to shape.
Winkel TripelVery low overall distortion; used by National Geographic.Curved edges leave empty corners on a 16:9 canvas.
MercatorZoomed-in regions, and matching web-map screenshots.Greenland looks as big as Africa. Never use it for a world map about size.
MillerA Mercator that tolerates the poles; fills a rectangle neatly.The same size distortion, only milder.
EquirectangularLining up with data grids; latitude and longitude are straight lines.Countries near the equator look squashed.
Globe (Orthographic)A single country or continent reveal; strong on 9:16.Only half the world is visible, by design.

8. Five recipes

A YouTube thumbnail that reads at small sizes

Dark preset, one group, a single bright colour such as yellow. Search the country, press Zoom to selection, then lower the zoom until the country fills about a third of the frame. Turn labels off (your thumbnail text does that job) and export 16:9 at 1×. High contrast and one focal point survive being shown 200 pixels wide.

An alliance or trade-bloc comparison

Atlas or Light preset. Group 1 “Members”, group 2 “Candidates”, group 3 “Partners”, each in a clearly different colour. Choose the region preset that holds them all, turn labels on and export at 2×. Keep the share link, so next year’s changes are a two-minute edit.

A globe reveal for a Short

Canvas 9:16, projection Globe, one highlighted country, Zoom to selection, then zoom out a touch so the curve of the globe shows. Export at 2× (2160 × 3840) and push in slowly in your editor for motion.

A printable classroom map

Paper or Light preset, grid lines on, labels on, canvas 4:3. Export SVG: it prints sharp at A3 and scales to a projector without blur. For a blank quiz map, leave the groups empty and turn labels off.

A transparent overlay for a video edit

Tick Transparent ocean/background and switch off the globe outline. The PNG then holds only land and borders, ready to lay over drone footage, a gradient or a satellite image.

9. Using the map in your editor

  • Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, Final Cut, CapCut: import the PNG like any image. A 2× export on a 1080p timeline leaves room to push in to 200% without softness.
  • After Effects: import the PNG for a still background. For shapes you can animate country by country, open the SVG in Illustrator, save it as an .ai file, import that, and use Create Shapes from Vector Layer.
  • Figma, Illustrator, Inkscape, Canva: open or drop in the SVG; each country stays a separate shape you can restyle.
  • PowerPoint, Keynote, Google Slides: paste straight from Copy PNG to clipboard, or insert the downloaded file.
